About this portfolio

Applied AI engineering is about turning models into dependable products. That work depends on clear fundamentals (control flow, state, abstraction, data structures) and on explaining those ideas to teammates, stakeholders, and users who did not write the code.

This course taught me to build that understanding in learners step by step, using low-cognitive-load scaffolding instead of "go write a program." The pieces below are my evidence.

  • Scaffolded lessons on core programming concepts
  • Recorded teaching and explanation videos
  • Pedagogy and equity practices for inclusive learning
  • Explicit links between each concept and AI engineering practice

Core concepts and where they show up in applied AI

Each card pairs a concept from the course with how it appears in real AI engineering work.

Sequences

Order of operations in a program.

In AI: data pipelines where cleaning, tokenizing, embedding, and inference must run in the right order.

Iteration

Repeating steps, including conditional loops.

In AI: training loops over batches and epochs, and retry or stop conditions in agent workflows.

Selection (ifs)

Choosing between paths based on a condition.

In AI: confidence thresholds, guardrails, and routing a request to the right model or tool.

Variables

Named values that hold and change state.

In AI: hyperparameters, conversation state, and model configuration.

Events

Code that responds to something happening.

In AI: webhooks, user actions triggering inference, and streaming responses.

Abstraction, methods, parameters

Naming a procedure and making it reusable.

In AI: reusable preprocessing functions, evaluation harnesses, and tool interfaces for models.

Lists

Ordered collections of items.

In AI: datasets, batches, token sequences, and retrieved documents.

Pedagogy & equity

Good teaching practice is also good engineering practice for diverse teams and users. Topics from this course I will document here:

  • Pair / buddy programming: shared ownership and real-time code review.
  • Peer instruction: learners test understanding by explaining to each other.
  • Culturally relevant teaching: connecting examples to learners' contexts.
  • State of K-12 CS education and standards: what is taught and what is missing.
  • Equitable lab classrooms: structures that give every learner access.
  • Interdisciplinary standards alignment: computing across subjects.

Reflection: teaching and AI engineering

Responsible AI work needs engineers who can explain what a system does and where it can fail. Practicing scaffolding, checking for misconceptions, and designing for equitable access all transfer to documenting models, onboarding teammates, and building tools that a wide range of people can use.
